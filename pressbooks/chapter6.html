Bayesian probability offers a way of thinking about uncertainty that goes beyond long-run frequency, treating probability more broadly as a measure of belief that can be updated as new evidence arrives. This chapter grew directly out of a tension in the previous one: classical hypothesis testing reduces inference to a binary decision governed by an arbitrary significance threshold, and its p-values, whatever their value, never directly tell us the probability that a hypothesis is true.

Reconsidering what probability actually means, and treating statistics as a mechanism for updating belief rather than simply rejecting hypotheses, is what defines the Bayesian approach. This reflects a genuine and long-standing philosophical divide: the frequentist view, tracing back to von Mises, ties probability to the limiting frequency of repeated trials, while the Bayesian and subjectivist tradition, rooted in the work of Thomas Bayes and later developed by Ramsey and de Finetti, treats probability as a coherent expression of rational belief.

Independent and Conditional Events

Before reasoning about how evidence should update our beliefs, it helps to be precise about what it means for one event to carry information about another. Two events are independent if learning that one has occurred tells us nothing new about the other, as with two separate coin tosses or two separate dice rolls, where knowing the outcome of one roll leaves our expectations about the other completely unchanged.

Many events are not independent in this way. Drawing a card from a deck without replacement, or drawing balls from an urn without replacement, are simple examples where the outcome of the first draw genuinely changes the odds for the second, since one item has already been removed from consideration. This distinction between independence and dependence, and the more general idea of conditional probability that lets us formally update a probability once new information is known, sets the stage for the central idea of the chapter.

The Bayes Rule

Conditional probability naturally raises an intriguing question: if we observe some evidence, how should we update our belief about its possible cause? This is what statisticians call an inverse problem, and Bayes' rule, tracing back to Thomas Bayes' posthumously published essay and later extended into a general framework by Laplace, provides its solution. The rule combines a prior belief about a hypothesis, a likelihood describing how probable the observed evidence would be under that hypothesis, and a marginal probability of the evidence overall, to produce a posterior belief, our updated view of the hypothesis once the evidence is taken into account.

A few classic examples make the logic vivid. Given a fair die and a biased die, observing a six should shift our belief meaningfully toward the biased one, precisely because a six is more likely under that hypothesis. In medical testing, a positive result on a highly accurate test can still leave real uncertainty if the underlying disease is rare, since the odds depend not just on the test's accuracy but also on how common the condition is to begin with.

And in the celebrated Monty Hall problem, switching doors after a host who knows where the prize is deliberately reveals an empty one turns out to double a contestant's chances of winning, a counterintuitive result that only becomes clear once we account for the fact that the host's action is not random but deliberately informative.

Frequentist vs Bayesian

These two schools of thought disagree less about the mathematics of probability than about what a probability statement actually means. The frequentist view treats an unknown parameter as a fixed, if unknown, constant, and randomness as something that lives entirely in the sampling process. Under this view, it is not meaningful to say a specific interval has a ninety-five percent chance of containing the true parameter, only that the procedure used to build such intervals would succeed ninety-five percent of the time if repeated indefinitely.

The Bayesian view instead treats the parameter itself as uncertain, described by a probability distribution that starts as a prior belief and gets sharpened into a posterior belief once data are observed, allowing statements about the probability of a specific value directly. Neither view is mathematically wrong; they simply anchor the meaning of probability in different places, hypothetical repetition on one side, rational belief on the other.

Bayesian Inference

Applying this belief-updating logic to real inference problems produces a strikingly consistent pattern across very different situations. To estimate the probability that a coin lands heads, we start with a prior distribution over that probability, observe a series of tosses, and combine the two into a posterior distribution that shifts toward whatever proportion of heads was actually observed, with the influence of the prior fading as more data accumulates.

To estimate an unknown population mean from repeated measurements, a similar logic applies. The posterior mean turns out to be a weighted average of the prior guess and the sample mean, with the weights reflecting how confident we were in each to begin with, and as the sample grows large, the data increasingly dominate that average, eventually overwhelming whatever the prior originally assumed.

The same pattern appears once more when estimating a rate of rare events, such as emails arriving per hour or defects on a production line, where the posterior estimate again lands between the prior guess and the observed rate, weighted according to how much data has actually been collected. In some of these cases, the mathematics works out so neatly that the posterior distribution takes exactly the same form as the prior, a convenient property known as conjugacy, which is part of why these particular examples appear so often when Bayesian methods are first introduced.
