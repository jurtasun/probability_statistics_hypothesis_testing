Linear algebra did not begin as a single unified theory. It grew out of a scattered collection of methods for solving systems of equations, describing geometry, and understanding transformations of space, and was only pulled together into the abstract, structural theory taught today in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. That shift from concrete computation to structural thinking is itself philosophically significant: rather than solving equations one at a time, the modern approach studies abstract objects such as vector spaces and the mappings between them, along with whatever properties stay invariant no matter how coordinates are chosen. This is precisely what allows the same handful of ideas to resurface across geometry, differential equations, probability, and statistical inference.

The history behind this abstraction spans many centuries and traditions. Gauss developed the systematic elimination methods still used to solve linear systems today, while Hermann Grassmann's 1844 treatise introduced the idea of an abstract vector space decades before mathematicians were ready to appreciate it. Arthur Cayley and James Joseph Sylvester built up matrix theory over the course of the nineteenth century, and David Hilbert and Emmy Noether later gave the field its modern structural and axiomatic character. A part of this story often gets underemphasized: the medieval Arabic mathematical tradition, and in particular Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi, working in ninth-century Baghdad during the Islamic Golden Age, whose treatise on solving linear and quadratic equations gave algebra both its name, from the Arabic term al-jabr, and its earliest systematic, rule-based methods for manipulating and classifying equations.

A working grasp of the subject rests on a handful of core ideas. Vectors and vector spaces formalize the notion of degrees of freedom: a vector space is simply a collection of objects that can be added together and scaled, and from this simple structure follow the ideas of linear combination, span, independence, basis, and dimension, which together describe how many independent directions a space really has. Matrices arise naturally as the concrete representation of linear transformations between such spaces, and much of introductory linear algebra is really about solving systems of linear equations efficiently using matrix operations, rank, and row reduction. Linear transformations themselves are simply functions between vector spaces that respect addition and scaling, and thinking in terms of these maps, rather than the specific numbers inside a matrix, is what clarifies the underlying geometry of a problem.

Beyond this algebraic backbone, a few additional structures do most of the practical work in statistics and applied mathematics. Inner products extend vector spaces with a notion of length, angle, and orthogonality, giving rise to projections and orthonormal bases that underpin least squares methods and regression. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors capture the directions along which a transformation simply stretches or shrinks space without rotating it, a concept that turns out to be central to everything from differential equations to principal component analysis. Determinants, while less emphasized in modern abstract treatments, still provide useful geometric intuition about volume, orientation, and whether a transformation can be undone at all. Taken together, these ideas form less a toolkit of isolated tricks than a coherent way of reasoning about structure, symmetry, and linearity, one that resurfaces constantly throughout the rest of this course.
