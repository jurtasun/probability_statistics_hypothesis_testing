Where differential calculus asks how a quantity changes at a single instant, integral calculus asks the complementary question: how do all those small, local changes add up to a total? How much area sits under a curve, how far does an object travel as its speed varies, how does a quantity spread across a range combine into a whole? The intuitive answer long predates modern calculus. In ancient Greece, Archimedes approximated areas and volumes by breaking them into many small pieces and summing the result, a method known as exhaustion that, though expressed purely geometrically, already captured the essential idea behind integration: a total quantity is simply the limit of increasingly fine approximations. Philosophically, this is the deeper project of integral calculus, understanding a whole as the sum of infinitely many parts, and replacing loose geometric intuition with rigorous, limit-based reasoning to make that understanding reliable.

This history, again, reaches well beyond Europe. The Kerala school in India developed infinite series and summation techniques for computing areas and trigonometric quantities, driven largely by the needs of astronomical calculation, while mathematicians in the medieval Arabic world refined methods for computing areas and volumes using geometric techniques closely related to what we now call integration, ideas that later entered Europe through translation. The more familiar chapter of the story, in which Newton and Leibniz independently formalized the fundamental principles of integral calculus in the late seventeenth century, still holds an important place: it was Leibniz who introduced the now-universal integral symbol, chosen precisely because it evoked the idea of summation. As with differentiation, this classical picture was later placed on far firmer footing in the nineteenth century, when Cauchy clarified the role of limits and convergence in integration, and Riemann gave the integral its now-standard rigorous definition in terms of partitioning an interval into ever-finer pieces.

The modern formalization simply makes Archimedes' intuition precise: divide an interval into many small pieces, add up the contribution of each piece, and take the limit as those pieces shrink toward zero width. What emerges from this limit is the definite integral, and one of the most remarkable facts in all of mathematics, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, shows that this accumulated total can actually be computed just by evaluating an antiderivative at the two endpoints of the interval, rather than by summing infinitely many pieces directly. In other words, knowing exactly how a quantity changes locally is, in a precise mathematical sense, enough to determine its total, global effect.

This connection between local behavior and global accumulation is exactly why integral calculus matters so much for probability and statistics. When a function represents a probability density, integrating it over some interval gives the total probability that a random variable falls within that range, translating a purely local description, how likely values are near any particular point, into a genuinely global statement about probability. This is not a minor technical convenience: it is the mathematical machinery that makes continuous probability distributions workable at all, and it is why integral calculus sits quietly underneath so much of the statistical reasoning developed throughout the rest of this course.
