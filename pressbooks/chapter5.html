Modern science rests on the idea that the natural world can be represented through variables whose relationships follow general mathematical laws. This way of thinking has deep roots, becoming explicit through Descartes' coordinate systems, which let geometric relationships be expressed algebraically, and through the calculus of Newton and Leibniz, which gave scientists the tools to describe continuous change through functions, derivatives, and integrals.

For a long time, this mathematization of nature was understood as fully deterministic: specify the input, and the output follows exactly. But as empirical science matured, it became clear that measurement error, unobserved influences, and genuine randomness meant that identical inputs could produce different outcomes. This tension between exact mathematical law and irreducible variability is what motivates the shift toward statistical modelling explored in this chapter.

Functions and Models

Different physical mechanisms tend to reveal themselves through different mathematical shapes, and part of the task of modelling is recognizing which shape fits a given process. Linear relationships describe situations where the response is proportional to the input, as in Hooke's law relating the force on a spring to its displacement. Quadratic relationships appear when the rate of change itself grows steadily, as with the position of an object under constant acceleration.

Sinusoidal functions capture oscillatory behavior, from springs and waves to electrical currents, and Fourier's insight that even highly irregular phenomena, such as the diffusion of heat, can be decomposed into sums of simple sine and cosine waves remains one of the most powerful ideas in applied mathematics. Exponential functions, finally, describe processes whose rate of change is proportional to their current state, as in population growth, electrical discharge, or radioactive decay.

In practice, though, real observations never fit these idealized curves exactly, and it is precisely this gap between theory and observation that calls for a statistical, rather than purely deterministic, approach.

Statistical Modelling

A statistical model formalizes this gap directly, writing an observed outcome as the sum of a systematic, deterministic component and a random error term that captures everything the deterministic part leaves unexplained. This is what distinguishes a stochastic model from a purely mathematical one: rather than assuming the relationship between two quantities holds exactly, we treat the observed variables as random, governed by some underlying probability law, with actual data representing particular realizations of that randomness.

This probabilistic reframing, developed through Laplace's interpretation of probability as a measure of uncertainty and later placed on rigorous footing by Kolmogorov's axioms, does not abandon the mathematical description of nature. It simply extends that description to accommodate the complexity and noise of real-world observation.

Linear Regression

The concept of regression traces back to Francis Galton, who noticed that unusually tall or short parents tend to have children closer to the average, a pattern he called regression toward mediocrity. This observation was later formalized into the modern statistical toolkit by Pearson and Fisher. The simplest version of this idea, simple linear regression, models an outcome as a baseline value plus a term that scales linearly with a single predictor, plus random error.

Fitting such a model means finding the line that makes the residuals, the gaps between observed and predicted values, as small as possible in aggregate, a principle known as the method of least squares, introduced by Legendre and later given a probabilistic justification by Gauss, who showed it follows naturally when errors are normally distributed.

The resulting estimates are only trustworthy, however, under four conditions: that the true relationship really is linear on average, that the errors for different observations are independent of one another, that the spread of the errors stays roughly constant across the range of the predictor, and that the errors themselves follow something close to a normal distribution.

Covariance and Correlation

Beyond fitting a specific model, it is often useful simply to ask how strongly two variables move together, an idea formalized by Pearson in the late nineteenth century. Covariance captures this joint variability directly: a positive value means the two variables tend to rise and fall together, a negative value means they tend to move in opposite directions, but the raw number depends awkwardly on the units in which each variable happens to be measured.

Correlation solves this problem by rescaling covariance into a number that always falls between minus one and one, regardless of units, making it far easier to interpret and compare across different pairs of variables. Because Pearson's coefficient only captures straight-line relationships, Spearman later introduced a rank-based alternative better suited to relationships that are consistently increasing or decreasing but not necessarily linear.

A now-famous cautionary example, Anscombe's quartet, drives the point home vividly: four data sets can share identical means, variances, and correlation coefficients while looking completely different once actually plotted, a reminder that numerical summaries should never fully replace visual inspection of the data.

Multiple Linear Regression

Real outcomes are rarely shaped by just one factor, which motivates extending the linear model to include several predictors at once, each with its own coefficient representing its effect while holding the other predictors fixed. Income might be modeled as a function of education, experience, and age together; the pressure of a gas as a function of both temperature and volume; disease risk as a function of several environmental and genetic factors simultaneously.

Written in matrix form, this extended model reveals a deep connection to linear algebra, since fitting it by least squares amounts geometrically to projecting the observed data onto the space spanned by the predictors.

Finally, it is worth being careful about a common point of confusion: the correlation coefficient captures both the direction and strength of a linear relationship, while its square, known as the coefficient of determination, discards that directional information and instead measures the proportion of variability in the outcome explained by the model. A high value of this squared quantity does not, on its own, imply that one variable causes changes in the other.
