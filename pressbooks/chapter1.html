Probability and statistics are both broad branches of mathematics addressing variability and uncertainty, which arise naturally when addressing natural phenomena. They have both deep historical roots, and they are commonly treated as interchangeable, while they address different questions. Probability is normally presented as predictive: it starts from a model of how the world behaves and rules it follows, and uses that model to anticipate the outcomes of random events.

Statistics, on the other hand, starts from observations and measurements, also referred to as data, a finite sample drawn from a much larger and usually inaccessible population, and asks what that data can tell us about the world that produced it. Both disciplines are concerned with uncertainty, but they approach it from opposite ends: one reasons from model to prediction, the other from observation to explanation. Keeping this distinction in mind is useful throughout the rest of the course, since many confusions in applied statistics come from mixing up the two.

Population and Sampling

Every statistical question begins with a population, the complete set of items, individuals, or observations that a study is actually interested in. In practice, this population is almost never fully accessible. We cannot survey every voter, measure every star, or test every patient, so researchers work instead with a sample: a smaller, finite subset that is actually collected and measured.

The quality of any conclusion drawn from data depends heavily on how that sample was chosen. A well-constructed sample mirrors the essential features of the population it is drawn from, while a poorly chosen one introduces bias, and can quietly distort every estimate built on top of it.

It is also worth pausing on the fact that not all data behaves the same way, and that this distinction shapes which tools are even appropriate to use. Categorical data describes qualities or labels rather than quantities. It can be nominal, meaning there is no natural order among the categories, as with eye colour or the brand of a phone, or ordinal, meaning the categories follow a meaningful sequence, as with race finishing positions or survey responses ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree.

Numerical data, in contrast, represents genuine quantities, and splits further into discrete data, which is countable, like the number of goals scored in a match, and continuous data, which can take any value within a range, like a person's height or the time it takes to run a hundred metres. Recognizing which kind of data one is working with is not a mere formality: as an example, computing a mean value would make sense for prices, but not for phone brands, etc.

These ideas have a long institutional history. Modern statistical practice owes a great deal to organizations such as the Royal Statistical Society, founded in 1834, whose early members included Adolphe Quetelet and Charles Babbage, both of whom pushed for quantitative, evidence-based approaches to social and administrative questions.

Among its most notable members was Florence Nightingale, admitted in 1858 as the Society's first female member, whose statistical graphics on wartime mortality remain a landmark example of using data to change policy. Their combined efforts helped move statistics away from being a curiosity of gamblers and dice throwers, and toward the formal discipline of inference that would be developed over the following century.

Central Tendency and Variation

A recurring difficulty in statistics is that the quantities we actually care about, the true population mean and the true population variance, are never directly observable. All we have is the sample. From this limited evidence, statisticians construct a quantity called the estimator: a numerical summary designed to approximate the properties of the population as closely as possible.

The most familiar of these is the sample mean, or average, which condenses an entire data set into a single typical value. It is intuitive and easy to compute, but it has a well-known weakness: it is highly sensitive to extreme values, so a single outlier can pull the mean far from where most of the data actually sits. The median offers a more robust alternative. Defined as the middle value of an ordered data set, it is largely unaffected by extreme observations, which makes it especially useful when data is skewed or contaminated by unusual values.

Central tendency, however, only tells half the story. Two data sets can share the exact same mean and still look completely different, depending on how spread out their values are. This is where measures of variation come in. The sample variance captures how far, on average, observations deviate from the mean, by summing their squared distances from it. Because squaring removes the sign of the deviation, the variance is always non-negative, and grows larger as the data becomes more dispersed.

The standard deviation, simply the square root of the variance, expresses that same spread in the original units of the data, which makes it easier to interpret intuitively. Quantiles round out this picture by dividing an ordered data set into proportions, with the quartiles in particular marking off the lower, central, and upper regions of a distribution. Taken together, these measures of central tendency and variation form the basic descriptive vocabulary that almost every later statistical method builds upon.

Data Visualization

Numbers alone can be difficult to absorb, and this is precisely where visualization earns its place alongside numerical summaries. The human eye tends to recognize patterns, shapes, and irregularities far faster than the mind can process a table of figures. As an archetypical example, the histogram is one of the oldest tools for this purpose, formalized by Karl Pearson in the late nineteenth century.

It works by dividing the data into intervals, or bins, and representing the number of observations that fall into each one as the height of a bar. Done well, a histogram gives an almost immediate sense of a distribution's symmetry, skewness, and any gaps or clusters in the data. Choosing the bin width, however, involves a genuine trade-off: narrow bins reveal more detail but can introduce noise and spurious fluctuations, particularly when outliers are present, while wider bins smooth the picture at the cost of some resolution.

Later refinements built on this same idea. The box plot, introduced by John Tukey around 1970 as part of his broader programme of exploratory data analysis, condenses a data set into five key statistics: the minimum, the first quartile, the median, the third quartile, and the maximum. It is compact, easy to compare across groups, and particularly good at flagging outliers without being distorted by them.

The violin plot, which emerged later in the twentieth century, builds on the same foundation but adds a smoothed density trace, so that shapes such as skewness or multiple peaks, features that a simple box plot can obscure, become visible at a glance.

It would be a mistake to treat visualization as mere decoration or presentation. It functions as a genuine safeguard against misinterpretation: data sets can share nearly identical means, variances, and correlations while looking entirely different once plotted, a fact that has surprised generations of students and researchers alike.

This tradition of representing data graphically has deep roots, tracing back to Descartes' introduction of coordinate systems in the seventeenth century, and carried forward by figures such as William Playfair, Adolphe Quetelet, Florence Nightingale, and Charles Joseph Minard, each of whom, in their own way, turned visualization into a central instrument of scientific and public reasoning.
