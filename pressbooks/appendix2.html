Calculus begins with a simple question: how does one quantity depend on another, and how does it change? The function is the concept that formalizes this dependence, and like so many mathematical ideas, it did not arrive fully formed. Descartes' coordinate geometry allowed curves to be expressed as algebraic equations, an implicit first step, before Leonhard Euler, in the eighteenth century, gave the function concept its modern clarity and much of the notation still used today. It took until the nineteenth century, through the work of Augustin-Louis Cauchy, for calculus to be placed on a genuinely rigorous footing, replacing intuitive appeals to infinitesimals with precise definitions built on the idea of a limit. That shift, from informal reasoning about continuous change to careful, finite argument, marks the transition from classical calculus to what we now call mathematical analysis.

This history is not exclusively European. Centuries before Newton and Leibniz, mathematicians of the Kerala school in India developed infinite series expansions for trigonometric functions and sophisticated approximation techniques, driven by the practical demands of astronomical calculation. Medieval Arabic mathematicians, meanwhile, refined algebraic methods and developed early geometric approaches to tangents and maxima, ideas that were later absorbed into European mathematics through translation. The familiar story crediting Newton and Leibniz with independently inventing calculus in the late seventeenth century is true as far as it goes, with Newton emphasizing its physical, motion-based interpretation and Leibniz contributing the flexible symbolic notation still in use today, but it is really the culmination of a much longer and more geographically distributed line of thought.

The idea of a limit sits at the conceptual heart of all of this. Long before it had a rigorous definition, the notion of approaching a value without necessarily reaching it troubled ancient Greek philosophers, most famously in Zeno's paradoxes about motion and infinite divisibility, and Archimedes worked around these difficulties using ingenious geometric arguments rather than formal limits. It was Cauchy who finally replaced this geometric intuition with a precise, arbitrarily-small-error definition of what it means for a function to approach a value, a treatment later sharpened further into the now-standard formal definition. Philosophically, the limit is what allows mathematics to reason about an idealized, never-quite-completed process using only finite, precise statements, reconciling the infinite with logical rigor.

The derivative is where this machinery becomes concrete. Built directly from the concept of a limit, it captures the instantaneous rate of change of a function at a single point, which can be read geometrically as the slope of the tangent line at that point, or physically as a quantity like velocity or growth rate. In this way, the derivative links two seemingly different ideas, an algebraic limiting process and a geometric picture of a curve, into a single, unified concept, and it is this bridge between computation and intuition that makes calculus such a powerful tool throughout probability, statistics, and the sciences more broadly.
