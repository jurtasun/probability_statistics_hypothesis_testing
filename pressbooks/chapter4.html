Hypothesis testing sits on top of everything covered so far: the descriptive tools of sampling and estimation, and the probability models used to represent random variation. Working mostly under the assumption that data follows a simple, well-behaved distribution, most often the Gaussian, we now ask how to formally compare what we observe against what a given hypothesis would predict. The central device for doing this is the test statistic, a number computed from data and designed to measure how far our observations sit from what we would expect if a particular hypothesis were true.

That comparison is usually summarized through the p-value, which quantifies how unusual the observed data would be under the assumption that the hypothesis holds. These ideas took shape gradually over the early twentieth century, through the work of Karl Pearson, Ronald Fisher, and later Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson, and their interpretation, as we will see, is not purely a technical matter but a genuinely philosophical one.

Prediction vs Inference Revisited

Every hypothesis test rests on the same underlying distinction discussed earlier in the course: an idealized population, characterized by true but inaccessible parameters, and a finite sample, from which we compute estimators such as the sample mean and sample variance. Mathematical prediction concerns what a model implies about those population-level parameters, whether that model is Newton's second law predicting the position of a falling object, a biological hypothesis about gene expression, or an epidemiological claim linking smoking to cancer risk.

In every one of these cases, the hypothesis is a statement about the population, while our only access to it runs through the finite, noisy evidence of a sample. Hypothesis testing formalizes exactly this comparison, asking how compatible an observed sample is with a hypothesized population-level model.

The General Approach

Stripped of its many variations across fields and eras, the now-standard approach to hypothesis testing follows a fairly consistent sequence of steps. One begins by formulating a null hypothesis, representing the reference or default case, and an alternative hypothesis, representing a departure that would be considered scientifically meaningful. Data is then collected through some experiment or measurement process, and a test statistic is computed from that data, quantifying how far the observations deviate from what the null hypothesis would predict.

From this statistic, a p-value is calculated: the probability, assuming the null hypothesis is true, of obtaining a result at least as extreme as the one actually observed. Finally, that p-value is compared against a chosen threshold to reach some conclusion, typically phrased as rejecting or failing to reject the null hypothesis.

It is worth being explicit about a common misunderstanding here: a p-value does not measure the probability that a hypothesis is true or false. It measures only how compatible the observed data are with the null hypothesis, under the assumptions of the model being used.

A Family of Statistical Tests

A number of classical tests apply this general logic to specific questions. The one-sample t-test, developed in 1908 by William Gosset while working at the Guinness brewery under the pseudonym "Student," compares an observed sample mean against a hypothesized value, accounting for the extra uncertainty that comes from not knowing the true population variance. The two-sample t-test extends this same idea to compare the means of two independent groups.

Fisher's F-test takes a different angle, comparing the variances of two groups rather than their means, and it generalizes naturally into the analysis of variance, or ANOVA, which compares the means of more than two groups at once by weighing the variability between groups against the variability within them. Pearson's chi-square test, introduced in 1900, addresses yet another kind of question, measuring how well observed counts of categorical data agree with what a model or an assumption of independence would predict.

A more general framework, the Wald test, developed by Abraham Wald in the 1930s, unifies many of these ideas by working directly with the large-sample behavior of estimators, and shows that familiar procedures like the z-test emerge as special cases of a single, more abstract approach applicable to a wide range of statistical models, including the kind of large-scale analyses used today in fields like genomics. What ties all of these tests together is not their specific formulas but their shared structure: a hypothesis about some population quantity, a statistic that captures the discrepancy between data and that hypothesis, and a known probability distribution that tells us how surprising the observed value of that statistic really is.

Parametric and Non-Parametric Tests

The tests described so far are parametric: they assume the data follows a distribution described by a small number of parameters, often the Gaussian. This assumption, while convenient and historically foundational, does not always hold in practice, which motivated the development of non-parametric alternatives, mostly during the 1940s and 1950s, that remain valid under much weaker assumptions about the shape of the data.

Where a one-sample or paired t-test assumes normality, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test achieves a similar goal by working with the ranks of the data instead. Where the two-sample t-test compares means directly, the Mann-Whitney U test compares two groups using ranks alone, without assuming any particular distribution.

Levene's test offers a more robust alternative to Fisher's F-test for comparing variability across groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test extends this rank-based logic to comparisons across more than two groups in place of ANOVA, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test compares an entire empirical distribution against a theoretical one, and the Shapiro-Wilk test directly assesses whether a sample plausibly came from a normal distribution in the first place, an assumption so many other tests quietly depend on.

Two Ways of Thinking About Error

Beneath these techniques lies a genuine and long-running philosophical disagreement about what a hypothesis test actually accomplishes. Fisher conceived of the p-value as a flexible, continuous measure of evidence against a null hypothesis, informing scientific judgment rather than triggering an automatic decision. Neyman and Pearson, by contrast, framed testing as a decision procedure: given two competing hypotheses, one designs a rule that controls the long-run rates of two distinct kinds of mistake, false positives and false negatives, and then simply follows that rule.

These two views do not agree on what error even means, since for Fisher it is a tail probability under a single model, while for Neyman and Pearson it is a long-run operating characteristic of a repeated decision procedure. Bayesian inference sidesteps this dichotomy altogether, treating probability as a degree of belief that gets updated by evidence, so that hypotheses themselves can be assigned probabilities and compared directly, rather than accepted or rejected against a fixed threshold.

These statistical debates connect to a broader current in twentieth-century philosophy of science. Reichenbach distinguished predictions about long-run frequencies from claims about truth or belief, clarifying why frequentist testing can operate without ever assigning a probability to a hypothesis itself. Popper went further still, arguing that science advances not by accumulating supportive evidence but through bold conjectures and serious attempts to prove them wrong, a view largely incompatible with the Bayesian idea of confirmation.

What emerges from all of this is not confusion so much as genuine plurality: the hypothesis testing procedure taught in most modern courses is a practical blend of ideas from traditions that were never fully compatible in the first place, and appreciating that history is essential to using these tools thoughtfully rather than mechanically.
