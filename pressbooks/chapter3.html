This chapter connects the descriptive tools of the earlier chapters with the probability models introduced afterward. Estimation is the process of using data to learn about unknown features of a population, and probability supplies the language needed to say how reliable that learning is. A useful distinction runs through the whole discussion: prediction is about forecasting future observations, while inference is about drawing conclusions about the underlying mechanism that produced the data in the first place.

Because different samples give different estimates, it is not enough to look at a single computed number. Instead, we need to think of an estimator itself as a random quantity, one with its own distribution across all the samples we might have drawn, and ask how that distribution behaves.

The Law of Large Numbers

The first of two foundational results addressing this question is the Law of Large Numbers, first proved by Jacob Bernoulli. Informally, it says that although any single observation may be highly variable, the average of many such observations becomes increasingly stable as more data is collected, so that randomness, in a sense, averages itself out. Toss a fair coin a few times and the proportion of heads can swing wildly, but toss it thousands of times and that proportion settles reliably near one half.

The same logic applies to repeated physical measurements: individual readings may differ slightly due to random error, but their average converges toward the true value as the number of measurements grows. This simple idea underlies the basic intuition of statistical estimation, that larger samples give more trustworthy information about the quantities we are trying to learn.

The Central Limit Theorem

The Law of Large Numbers tells us where an average is heading, but not how it fluctuates on the way there. That is the role of the Central Limit Theorem, whose roots trace back to Abraham de Moivre's observation that binomial outcomes can be approximated by a normal curve, and which was extended by Laplace into a much more general statement.

The theorem says that if you take the average of many independent observations, however irregular or skewed those individual observations might be, the distribution of that average will tend to look approximately bell-shaped, centered on the true mean, and narrowing as the sample size grows. A student's daily study hours might vary unevenly from day to day, but the average study time computed over many separate months will cluster in a roughly Gaussian pattern around the true average.

This result explains why the normal distribution appears so persistently throughout statistics, and together with the Law of Large Numbers it provides both a guarantee that estimates converge and a description of how they fluctuate along the way.

Bias, Variance, and Measuring Spread

Since any estimator is itself a random variable, it makes sense to ask how good it is as a recipe for guessing an unknown quantity. Two different things can go wrong. An estimator can be systematically off, consistently too high or too low, which is called bias, or it can be erratic, swinging widely from sample to sample even if it is correct on average, which is called variance.

A helpful way to picture the distinction is a dartboard: an unbiased but high-variance thrower scatters darts all around the bullseye, while a low-variance but biased thrower groups their darts tightly, just in the wrong spot. These two sources of error combine into a single measure of overall estimation quality, which captures how far, on average, the estimator strays from the truth, accounting for both its systematic offset and its random spread together.

Applying this lens to familiar quantities is instructive. The sample mean turns out to be an unbiased estimator of the population mean, and its variance shrinks in proportion to the sample size, exactly as the Law of Large Numbers would suggest.

The sample variance is a subtler case. If we naively measure how spread out the data is around the sample mean itself, rather than around the unknown true mean, we systematically underestimate the true variability, because the sample mean is, by construction, the value that makes the data look as tightly clustered as possible. Correcting for this requires dividing by one less than the sample size rather than by the sample size itself, a fix known as Bessel's correction, which can also be understood through the idea of degrees of freedom: having already used the data once to estimate the mean, only n minus one independent pieces of information remain to estimate the spread.

A closely related quantity, the sample covariance, extends this same logic to pairs of variables, measuring whether two quantities, such as hours studied and exam scores, tend to move together or in opposite directions, and it requires the same correction for exactly the same reason.

Confidence Intervals and Critical Regions

A single point estimate, however carefully computed, says nothing on its own about how much to trust it. Confidence intervals address this by providing a range of plausible values for an unknown parameter, together with a precise statement about how often such ranges succeed in capturing the truth under repeated sampling.

It is worth being careful about what this actually means: a ninety-five percent confidence interval does not mean there is a ninety-five percent chance that the true value lies within the particular interval we happened to compute. The true value is fixed, even if unknown. What is random is the interval itself, and the ninety-five percent refers to how often the procedure would succeed if repeated many times over.

Because sample means become approximately normal for large samples, these intervals can be built directly from standard reference points: roughly sixty-eight percent of outcomes fall within one standard deviation of the mean, about ninety-five percent within two, and nearly ninety-nine point seven percent within three, with wider intervals trading precision for reliability.

Some fields demand far stronger evidence before drawing conclusions. In particle physics, a result strong enough to occur by chance only a few times in a thousand is considered merely suggestive evidence, while a genuine discovery requires a result so unlikely that it would occur by chance roughly once in a few million. This stringent standard was famously met in July 2012, when physicists at CERN announced the discovery of the Higgs boson, with two independent experiments each reporting results well beyond that extraordinary threshold.

Finally, confidence intervals and hypothesis tests turn out to be two expressions of the same underlying idea: a confidence interval contains exactly those values that a hypothesis test would fail to reject, so building an interval and running a test are, in a formal sense, the same calculation viewed from two different angles, a connection the next chapter develops more fully.
