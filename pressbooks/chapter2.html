Probability is, at its core, a branch of mathematics developed to describe random, also referred to as stochastic, phenomena, and to give a numerical language to address uncertainty. Its modern development began in the seventeenth century with the famous correspondence between Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat over games of chance, and was carried forward by Jacob Bernoulli's early treatment of probability and by later refinements from Abraham de Moivre and Pierre-Simon Laplace.

For a long time, however, probability lacked a fully rigorous foundation. That changed in 1933, when Andrey Kolmogorov showed that probability could be built on measure theory, using a small set of axioms from which the rest of the theory follows, independent of how one chooses to interpret it philosophically.

That question of interpretation matters, because two influential views of what probability actually means emerged in parallel. The frequentist view, developed most clearly by Richard von Mises, identifies probability with the long-run relative frequency of an event across many repeated trials: a fair coin has probability one half of landing heads because, over many tosses, roughly half will do so. And as we increase the number of observations, or sample size, we expect that value to asymptotically approach a perfect half.

The Bayesian view, tracing back to Thomas Bayes and developed further by Laplace and later mathematicians, instead treats probability as a subjective degree of belief, one that gets updated as new evidence becomes available. Both views rely on the same underlying mathematics; they differ only in what a probability statement is supposed to mean.

Discrete Events

Once probability is placed on this axiomatic footing, it becomes useful to classify random events by how their probabilities are distributed. Discrete events are those with a finite or countable set of possible outcomes, and their probabilities are described by mass functions that assign an exact probability to each individual outcome.

The simplest case is the Bernoulli trial, formalized by Jacob Bernoulli, which models any experiment with exactly two outcomes, such as a coin landing heads or tails, or a treatment succeeding or failing. Repeating such a trial many times under identical conditions and counting the successes leads to the binomial distribution, studied systematically by Abraham de Moivre, which describes situations like the number of defective items in a batch or the number of patients responding to a treatment.

Two further discrete models round out this picture. The discrete uniform distribution formalizes the idea that, absent any distinguishing information, all outcomes should be treated as equally likely, as with the roll of a fair die. The Poisson distribution, introduced by Siméon Denis Poisson while studying rare events in judicial statistics, describes the number of occurrences of an event within a fixed interval of time or space, and is well suited to phenomena such as calls arriving at a switchboard or decay events detected by a sensor.

Underlying all of these is the cumulative distribution function, which answers a slightly different question than the mass function: not how likely is this exact outcome, but how likely is an outcome at or below a given value. It is non-decreasing, always between zero and one, and it provides a single, unified way of describing how probability accumulates along the real line.

Continuous Events

Continuous events pose a subtler problem. When outcomes can take any value along an interval of real numbers, such as a temperature measurement or a waiting time, the probability of any single exact value is mathematically zero, even though the outcome itself is perfectly possible. To resolve this, probability is described instead through a density function, and actual probabilities are obtained by looking at the area under that density over a range of values, rather than at any single point.

The best known of these is the Gaussian, or normal, distribution, whose physical interpretation was given by Carl Friedrich Gauss in the context of astronomical measurement error. It describes phenomena that result from the accumulation of many small, independent effects, from measurement noise to biological traits such as height, and its central importance in probability theory stems from the central limit theorem, covered later in the course.

Other continuous models capture different kinds of uncertainty. The exponential distribution, connected to Poisson's work on rare events, models the waiting time until something happens, such as the time until a machine fails or the next customer arrives, and is characterized by a distinctive lack of memory: how long you have already waited tells you nothing about how much longer you will wait.

The continuous uniform distribution, meanwhile, represents complete ignorance about where within a bounded interval an outcome might fall, making it a natural neutral reference model, often used in simulation and random sampling. As in the discrete case, a cumulative distribution function can be defined for continuous variables as well, only now it is obtained by integrating the density rather than summing probabilities, and it plays the same unifying role connecting probability to the tools of calculus.

Expected Values

Beyond describing distributions in full, it is often enough to summarize them with a handful of representative numbers, known as moments. The most fundamental of these is the expected value, or mean, which represents the long-run average outcome of an experiment repeated many times, and answers the simple question of where a distribution is centered. It should not be confused with the sample mean discussed in the previous chapter: one is a property of a random variable itself, the other is a number computed from an actual, finite set of observations.

The second moment of interest is the variance, which measures how spread out a distribution is around its mean, by looking at the expected squared distance between outcomes and that mean. Higher moments exist too, capturing asymmetry and the heaviness of a distribution's tails, but in practice the mean and variance already provide a remarkably powerful summary, and most of classical statistical inference is built directly on estimators of these two quantities.

Each of the distributions introduced in this chapter has its own characteristic mean and variance, and comparing them is instructive. For a Bernoulli trial, the mean is simply the success probability itself, while the variance is largest when that probability is exactly one half. For the binomial distribution, both the mean and the variance scale directly with the number of trials. The Poisson distribution has the curious property that its mean and variance are always equal to one another.

The Gaussian distribution is defined directly in terms of its mean and variance, since these are precisely the two parameters that shape it, while for the exponential distribution, a larger rate parameter corresponds to a shorter expected waiting time and less variability around it. Finally, both the discrete and continuous uniform distributions have a mean sitting exactly at the midpoint of their range, with a variance that depends only on how wide that range is. Together, these examples show how a great deal of the shape and behaviour of a distribution can already be read off from just two numbers.
