Almost everything covered so far has quietly assumed independence: samples drawn without influencing one another, errors uncorrelated, the past irrelevant to the future. Real sequences and natural phenomena, though, rarely work this way. Tomorrow's weather depends on today's, a stock price this afternoon depends on its price this morning, and the next word in a sentence depends on the words that came before it. This chapter traces how probability theory learned to handle dependence, and the story begins, unexpectedly, with a dispute about free will in late Imperial Russia.

The mathematician Pavel Nekrasov argued that the law of large numbers required independent events, and concluded from this that stable social patterns, such as crime statistics, were indirect proof that individuals exercise genuine free will, entangling mathematics with conservative theology. Andrei Markov, an outspoken atheist and critic of the tsarist establishment, set out to prove Nekrasov wrong.

He did so by painstakingly counting vowels and consonants letter by letter through the opening of Pushkin's novel Eugene Onegin. What he found was that this manifestly dependent sequence still exhibited the same stable long-run regularities the law of large numbers predicts for independent ones. Independence, it turned out, was never necessary, only convenient, and out of this literary rebuttal came one of the most consequential ideas in all of applied mathematics. It would even lay the ground, years later, to the founding blocks of generative artificial intelligence.

Markov Chains

The abstraction Markov performed, keeping only the classification of each letter as a vowel or consonant and the probabilities of moving between the two, generalizes far beyond poetry. A Markov chain is any sequence of states where the probability of the next state depends only on the current one, not on the path taken to get there, a property known as the Markov property. This is not a claim that the past is irrelevant; it is a claim that everything relevant about the past is already summarized in the present state, whether that state represents a letter, a weather condition, or something far more abstract.

The full behavior of such a system is captured compactly by a table of transition probabilities, and questions about what happens many steps into the future, not just one, can be answered by systematically combining these one-step probabilities. States themselves can be classified by their long-run behavior: some are visited again and again indefinitely, others are eventually abandoned for good, and some, once entered, cannot be left at all. Under the right conditions, a chain settles into a stationary pattern in which the proportion of time spent in each state stops changing however much longer the system runs, a striking analogue of the law of large numbers for dependent sequences, and precisely the mathematical fact that let Markov defeat Nekrasov's argument.

This same machinery turns out to explain a great deal in the world. It shows why a persistent gambler playing a perfectly fair game against a wealthier opponent is nonetheless certain to go broke eventually, and, in a rather different register, it is the mathematical core of Google's original PageRank algorithm, where a page's importance is simply how often a random web surfer following links would end up there in the long run.

Continuous Time and Hidden States

Not every process naturally advances in discrete steps. A radioactive atom decays at some random continuous moment, not on a fixed schedule, and forcing such processes into artificial discrete time steps distorts them. Continuous-time Markov chains handle this by working directly with rates of transition rather than fixed-step probabilities, recovering, for instance, the familiar exponential decay law as the average behavior of many individually random atomic decays.

The same continuous-time framework underlies models of disease spread, where randomness can explain the chance extinction of an outbreak in a way a purely deterministic model cannot, and models of how proteins flicker between different physical shapes. In every case, the point is the same: treating time as continuous, rather than forcing it into artificial steps, gives a more faithful picture of how the underlying process actually behaves.

A separate and equally important extension addresses situations where the true state of a system cannot be observed directly, only some noisy signal generated by it, as when a doctor sees symptoms rather than the disease itself, or a listener hears sound rather than the words intended. These hidden Markov models introduce a genuinely hidden layer of Markovian dynamics beneath the observed data, and learning to infer that hidden layer from observations became one of the most influential frameworks in probabilistic modelling, long before it found its modern echo in speech recognition and language technology.

Monte Carlo and Simulation

A completely different use for randomness emerged from a hospital bed in wartime Los Alamos, where Stanislaw Ulam, recovering from illness and idly playing solitaire, realized that some quantities too hard to calculate directly could simply be estimated by simulating a process many times over and averaging the results. This Monte Carlo idea, refined with John von Neumann, converges to the right answer regardless of how many dimensions the underlying problem has, which makes it indispensable for the kind of high-dimensional integrals that appear constantly in modern statistics and physics.

The difficulty is that drawing independent samples from a complicated target distribution, such as a Bayesian posterior, is often itself infeasible. Markov chains supply the fix: rather than sampling independently, one designs a chain whose long-run stationary distribution is exactly the distribution of interest, and simply lets that chain run, since after enough steps the states it visits behave like samples from the target.

This family of Markov chain Monte Carlo methods, including the classic Metropolis-Hastings algorithm and the closely related Gibbs sampler, quietly underlies an enormous share of modern applied Bayesian statistics. It also reveals a deep and somewhat beautiful structural link between statistical inference and the physics of systems in thermal equilibrium, since both are expressed through the same family of exponential probability distributions.

From Markov Chains to Transformers and Modern Machine Learning

The most direct descendants of Markov's original idea reach much further than most students expect. A Markov decision process extends a Markov chain by adding an agent that chooses actions and receives rewards, seeking a strategy that maximizes cumulative reward over time, and the field of reinforcement learning is devoted to learning such strategies directly from experience. This framework, resting fundamentally on the same convergence guarantees that let Markov settle his dispute with Nekrasov, underlies systems that learned to play the game of Go at a superhuman level purely through self-play, and it is also the mechanism by which today's conversational AI systems are fine-tuned using human feedback as a reward signal.

Recurrent neural networks took a parallel but distinct path, maintaining a continuously updated internal summary of everything seen so far in a sequence, conceptually playing the same role as the state in a Markov chain but learned from data rather than specified by hand, an idea later refined to handle much longer sequences. The transformer architecture, which now underlies most large language models, abandons the Markov assumption outright, letting predictions depend on an entire history rather than just the immediately preceding state, yet it still frames language generation as exactly the kind of sequential, probabilistic process that Markov first formalized while counting letters in a Russian poem.

Even the diffusion models behind much of today's image generation are, at their core, Markov chains: a forward chain that gradually turns a real image into pure noise, and a learned process that reverses that chain step by step to generate something new. A dispute over free will in Imperial Russia, it turns out, laid the mathematical groundwork for a great deal of what now looks like artificial intelligence.
